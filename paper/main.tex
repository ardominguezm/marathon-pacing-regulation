\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Purpose} Marathon split times are routinely collected under ecologically valid conditions, yet most pacing metrics compress the trajectory into global variability, categorical profiles, or early-to-late ratios. We developed an order-sensitive functional of local pacing regulation and determined the earliest race distance at which the observed trajectory contains reproducible information about subsequent late-race capacity preservation.

\textbf{Methods} We analyzed 5-km split records from 551,337 finishers of the Berlin, Boston, Chicago, and New York City Marathons across 16 race-year cohorts. Segment velocity was normalized to an early-race reference velocity. Horizon-specific pacing regulation cost (APRC) was defined as the mean squared change between successive normalized segment velocities observed through 15, 20, or 25 km. The primary outcome was late-race capacity preservation over 30--40 km relative to early-race velocity. Models adjusted for early-race velocity, age, sex, marathon, and year. Validation included race-year replication, performance strata, spatial progression, comparison with coefficient of variation (CV), and a confirmatory analysis replacing the ratio outcome with absolute late-race velocity conditional on early velocity.

\textbf{Results} At 15 km, APRC added negligible information about subsequent capacity preservation ($\Delta R^2=0.00018$) and was negative in 11/16 cohorts. At 20 km, the association was negative in all 16 cohorts (standardized $\beta=-0.00977$, 95\% CI $-0.01032$ to $-0.00922$; $\Delta R^2=0.00939$), and strengthened at 25 km ($\beta=-0.02575$, 95\% CI $-0.02663$ to $-0.02487$; $\Delta R^2=0.06288$). APRC added information beyond CV at 20 km ($\Delta R^2=0.02771$) and 25 km ($0.04294$). The estimated information horizon was unchanged when the outcome was absolute 30--40-km velocity conditional on early velocity: essentially no incremental information was present at 15 km ($\Delta R^2=0.00001$), whereas associations were negative in 16/16 cohorts at 20 km ($\beta=-0.05014$, $\Delta R^2=0.00227$) and 25 km ($\beta=-0.12279$, $\Delta R^2=0.01311$).

\textbf{Conclusion} A transparent quadratic functional derived from routine split times captured the ordered local structure of marathon pacing. Across four major marathons, a reproducible information signal about subsequent late-race performance emerged by approximately 20 km and strengthened markedly by 25 km. This information horizon is an empirical property of the observed pacing signal, not a universal physiological threshold.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} marathon running; pacing; endurance performance; fatigue resistance; field methods; real-world data

\section{Introduction}

Pacing is the continuously evolving regulation of work rate across a finite exercise task. In marathon running, the realized velocity trajectory reflects the interaction of sustainable metabolic intensity, substrate availability, neuromuscular function, thermoregulatory strain, perception of effort, terrain, environmental conditions, tactical decisions, and the athlete's expectation of what can be sustained \citep{Abbiss2008,Renfree2018,Azevedo2021}. Because official timing mats repeatedly sample this trajectory under authentic competitive conditions, marathon races provide an unusual field setting in which a performance signal can be studied at population scale without adding instrumentation.

The methodological challenge is that most commonly used pacing descriptors discard much of this signal. Marathon pacing has traditionally been represented by positive, even, or negative profiles, early-to-late percentage changes, pace range, or the coefficient of variation (CV) of split velocity \citep{Sha2024,Ristanovic2026}. These measures have established robust population-level regularities. Faster runners generally exhibit more stable pacing, whereas larger fluctuations and stronger late-race slowing are more common at slower performance levels \citep{Sha2024,Ristanovic2026,Dong2026}. Sex- and age-associated differences have also been reported, although their interpretation is sensitive to performance level, race context, data quality, and analytical choices \citep{Cuk2020,Tenan2025}. Recent methodological criticism of real-world marathon analyses has specifically emphasized raw-data quality control, the limitations of ratio-based outcomes, and the danger of assigning biological mechanisms to statistical patterns without adequate validation \citep{Tenan2025}.

A parallel literature has moved toward richer representations of the endurance-performance signal. Recent work provides two particularly close methodological precedents: serial-dependence tracking of mile-by-mile pacing in the New York City Marathon \citep{Thuany2025}, and cross-edition analyses showing reproducible but course-specific pacing profiles \citep{Oficial2021,Oficial2022}. Large-scale studies have formalized late-race ``hitting the wall'' using split-derived deterioration rules \citep{Smyth2021,Doherty2020}; inertial-sensor studies have identified biomechanical changes preceding substantial pace reduction \citep{Meyer2021,Miyazaki2025}; and critical-speed and power-duration approaches provide physiological reference frames for sustainable intensity and durability \citep{SmythMuniz2020,Drake2024,WahlJi2026}. These approaches converge on an important idea: the \emph{organization} of performance across distance may contain information that is lost when the race is compressed into one global summary.

This issue is especially relevant to method development. The \emph{Methods in Applied Physiology} initiative has argued for high-quality records, physiology-based interpretation, and transparent assessment rather than analytical complexity for its own sake \citep{Westerblad2025}. Recent work in the European Journal of Applied Physiology illustrates how mathematical models can transform routinely obtainable performance observations into interpretable physiological or performance assessments, while making their assumptions explicit \citep{Drake2024,Briand2025,WahlJi2026}. Existing field approaches therefore quantify global dispersion, classify pacing profiles, track serial dependence, or identify deterioration once it develops; early-warning studies additionally use training-derived critical speed or biomechanical signals. To our knowledge, no previous split-time approach has defined an order-sensitive local regulation functional and used its progressive observation to determine a reproducible information horizon for subsequent marathon performance.

We therefore introduce a quadratic functional of normalized segment-to-segment velocity changes. The construction is analogous to a discrete roughness or Dirichlet-type functional: it is small when adjacent normalized velocities remain locally smooth and increases when the trajectory changes more sharply between successive segments. The word \emph{cost} is mathematical, not calorimetric. The functional is not an estimate of metabolic energy expenditure, oxygen consumption, mechanical work, or thermodynamic entropy production. This distinction is important because metabolic power can only be inferred from running velocity under additional assumptions about running cost, acceleration, gradient, and other determinants \citep{Briand2025}.

The primary aim was to determine whether this split-derived functional contains reproducible information about subsequent late-race capacity preservation. The second aim was to determine the earliest observation horizon at which this information becomes consistent across independent marathon-year cohorts---the \emph{information horizon}. We hypothesized that (i) greater early pacing-regulation cost would be associated with poorer subsequent capacity preservation; (ii) the information carried by the functional would strengthen as the observation horizon extended from 15 to 25 km; (iii) the association would replicate across races, years, and performance strata; and (iv) the ordered local functional would contain information beyond conventional CV. Finally, because ratio outcomes can generate mathematical coupling, we specified a confirmatory analysis using absolute late-race velocity conditional on early velocity.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study design and race records}
We conducted a retrospective observational method-development study using publicly available official race-result records from the Berlin, Boston, Chicago, and New York City Marathons. The database contained age, recorded sex, race year, marathon, finish time, and cumulative timing-mat observations at 5-km intervals. The analysis used naturally occurring field measurements rather than laboratory physiological recordings. Participant names were not used in statistical inference and are not redistributed in the reproducibility materials.

\subsection{Quality control}
Records were restricted to runners aged 15--80 years, finish times below 8 h, and recorded sex coded as female or male in the source data. Primary analyses required complete cumulative checkpoints from 5 through 40 km and monotonically increasing cumulative times. Segment velocities outside 1.5--7.5 m\,s$^{-1}$ were excluded, as were records with adjacent-segment velocity ratios outside 0.55--1.55. A stricter specification (1.8--7.0 m\,s$^{-1}$; adjacent ratio 0.65--1.40) was examined during method development. The primary quality-control pipeline retained 551,337 of 570,843 records (96.6\%).

\subsection{Segment velocity and early-race normalization}
For runner $i$ and 5-km segment $k$,
\begin{equation}
v_{ik}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t_{ik}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x=5$ km. Segment labels refer to their ending checkpoint; for example, $v_{i,10}$ is velocity from 5 to 10 km. We defined
\begin{equation}
v_{i,\mathrm{ref}}=\operatorname{median}(v_{i,10},v_{i,15},v_{i,20}),
\qquad
r_{ik}=\frac{v_{ik}}{v_{i,\mathrm{ref}}}.
\end{equation}
This normalization removes absolute speed scale from the local-change functional while retaining the ordered spatial pattern of relative velocity.

\subsection{Horizon-specific pacing regulation cost}
For observation horizon $x$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_i(x)=\frac{1}{m(x)}
\sum_{j=1}^{m(x)}
\left[r_i(x_{j+1})-r_i(x_j)\right]^2,
\label{eq:aprc}
\end{equation}
where $m(x)$ is the number of segment-to-segment transitions observed through $x$. Primary horizons were 15, 20, and 25 km. Thus, $\mathcal{R}_i(15)$ contains the 10--15-km transition; $\mathcal{R}_i(20)$ additionally contains the 15--20-km transition; and $\mathcal{R}_i(25)$ additionally contains the 20--25-km transition. We refer to $\mathcal{R}_i(x)$ as horizon-specific pacing regulation cost (APRC).

Squaring makes every local contribution non-negative and gives greater weight to larger adjacent changes. Averaging permits comparison across horizons containing different numbers of transitions. At a fixed horizon, APRC is proportional to the corresponding discrete sum of squared gradients. It is a trajectory-roughness functional and has no units of physiological energy.

\subsection{Primary outcome: late-race capacity preservation}
Late-race capacity preservation was defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{LCP}_i=
\frac{\overline v_{i,30-40}}
     {\overline v_{i,5-20}}.
\label{eq:lcp}
\end{equation}
Values closer to one indicate greater preservation of velocity, whereas lower values indicate larger late-race slowing. The 25-km primary observation horizon retains a 5-km spatial buffer before the 30--40-km outcome window. A 30-km horizon was evaluated only as a boundary sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Information horizon}
For each horizon, the baseline model included standardized $v_{\mathrm{ref}}$, age, age squared, sex, marathon, and year. APRC was then added as a standardized predictor. Incremental explanatory information was
\begin{equation}
\Delta R^2(x)=R^2_{\mathrm{baseline}+\mathcal R(x)}
-R^2_{\mathrm{baseline}}.
\end{equation}
The empirical information onset was operationally defined within the final analysis workflow as the earliest primary horizon satisfying three conditions: $\Delta R^2\ge0.005$, a negative standardized APRC coefficient, and negative coefficients in at least 75\% of the 16 marathon-year cohorts. This rule is a reproducibility criterion for the observed signal and not a physiological or clinical threshold.

\subsection{Validation framework}
Construct behavior was examined by comparing PRC across 10--20, 20--30, and 30--40 km. Replication was assessed by estimating the APRC association independently in each marathon-year cohort. Robustness across performance level was examined in finish-time quartiles defined within sex, marathon, and year. To determine whether ordered local changes contained information beyond global variability, we compared models containing same-horizon CV with models containing CV plus APRC. Because APRC and CV are strongly correlated, mutually adjusted coefficient signs were not interpreted mechanistically; the comparison focused on incremental $R^2$.

\subsection{Confirmatory outcome de-coupling analysis}
The primary LCP outcome in Eq.~\ref{eq:lcp} contains early velocity in its denominator, while APRC is normalized by an early-race reference velocity. To test whether the information horizon could be produced by mathematical coupling, we performed a final confirmatory analysis in which the outcome was absolute mean velocity over 30--40 km,
\begin{equation}
Y_i=\overline v_{i,30-40},
\end{equation}
and early reference velocity was entered explicitly as a covariate. For $x\in\{15,20,25\}$,
\begin{equation}
z(Y_i)=\alpha+\beta_x z[\mathcal R_i(x)]
+\gamma z(v_{i,\mathrm{ref}})
+f(\mathrm{age})+\mathrm{sex}+\mathrm{marathon}+\mathrm{year}+\varepsilon_i.
\end{equation}
The confirmatory pattern was considered robust if the APRC coefficient was negative at 20 and 25 km, at least 75\% of race-year cohorts were negative at both horizons, and incremental $R^2$ increased from 15 to 20 to 25 km.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Continuous predictors used for effect-size comparison were standardized. Ordinary least-squares models were fitted with heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 standard errors. Results are reported as standardized regression coefficients, 95\% confidence intervals, and incremental $R^2$. With a sample exceeding half a million observations, statistical significance alone was not considered evidence of practical information; interpretation emphasized effect magnitude, incremental explanatory information, spatial ordering, and replication. Analyses were conducted in Python using pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, SciPy, and Matplotlib.

\subsection{Ethics and reporting}
The study used secondary publicly available race-result data and involved no participant contact or intervention. Identifiable names were not used for inference and are not distributed with the analytical materials. The formal institutional determination regarding ethics review or exemption will be inserted before submission. Reporting was informed by STROBE principles for observational studies.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Analytical cohort and spatial progression of regulation cost}
The quality-control pipeline retained 551,337 of 570,843 records (96.6\%) across four marathons and 16 race-year cohorts. PRC increased with race progression. Mean values were 0.00186 over 10--20 km, 0.00328 over 20--30 km, and 0.00381 over 30--40 km; corresponding medians were 0.00045, 0.00127, and 0.00169. Median late-race PRC was therefore approximately 3.75 times the early-race value (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}).

\subsection{The pacing trajectory became reproducibly informative by approximately 20 km}
At 15 km, APRC showed a statistically resolved but practically small association with subsequent LCP (standardized $\beta=-0.00131$, 95\% CI $-0.00164$ to $-0.00098$), adding only $\Delta R^2=0.00018$. Eleven of 16 race-year cohorts (68.8\%) had negative coefficients. Thus, pooled statistical detectability preceded reproducibility.

At 20 km, the pattern changed: $\beta=-0.00977$ (95\% CI $-0.01032$ to $-0.00922$), $\Delta R^2=0.00939$, and all 16 race-year cohorts had negative coefficients. At 25 km, the association strengthened to $\beta=-0.02575$ (95\% CI $-0.02663$ to $-0.02487$), with $\Delta R^2=0.06288$ and 16/16 directionally concordant cohorts (Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}). The operational information horizon was therefore 20 km.

The 30-km boundary analysis yielded a larger association ($\beta=-0.03494$, 95\% CI $-0.03615$ to $-0.03372$; $\Delta R^2=0.11752$), but because the horizon directly abutted the 30--40-km outcome window it was not used to define information onset.

\subsection{Replication across races, years, and performance strata}
At 15 km, race-year coefficients ranged from $-0.00512$ to $+0.00501$ (median $-0.00269$). At 20 km, all 16 estimates were negative (median $-0.01043$; range $-0.01478$ to $-0.00291$). At 25 km, all remained negative and increased in magnitude (median $-0.02340$; range $-0.03472$ to $-0.01328$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:replication}). Seven cohorts already had confidence intervals below zero at 15 km; the remaining nine reached this criterion by 20 km.

The horizon-dependent pattern also persisted across performance quartiles. In the fastest quartile, $\Delta R^2$ increased from 0.00126 at 15 km to 0.00897 at 20 km and 0.05493 at 25 km; standardized coefficients were $-0.00261$, $-0.00700$, and $-0.01763$. At 25 km, coefficients remained negative in all four performance strata (Fig.~\ref{fig:strata}).

\subsection{APRC contained information beyond conventional pacing variability}
APRC and same-horizon CV were strongly correlated ($r=0.81$, 0.83, and 0.82 at 15, 20, and 25 km, respectively), confirming that both measure aspects of pacing variability. Nevertheless, adding APRC to a model already containing CV increased $R^2$ by 0.00272 at 15 km, 0.02771 at 20 km, and 0.04294 at 25 km. Thus, the ordered local functional contained explanatory information not captured by global dispersion alone. Conditional coefficient signs were not interpreted because of collinearity.

\subsection{The information horizon survived removal of the ratio outcome}
The confirmatory outcome de-coupling analysis produced a sharper separation between the pre-information and information-bearing horizons (Table~\ref{tab:decouple}). The baseline model for absolute 30--40-km velocity already explained $R^2=0.80579$ after adjustment for early velocity and covariates. At 15 km, APRC added essentially no information ($\Delta R^2=0.000011$); the pooled coefficient was small and positive ($\beta=0.00335$, 95\% CI 0.00209 to 0.00460), only 8/16 cohorts were negative, and only 3/16 had confidence intervals entirely below zero.

At 20 km, APRC was negative in all 16 cohorts ($\beta=-0.05014$, 95\% CI $-0.05302$ to $-0.04725$; $\Delta R^2=0.002267$), with all 16 confidence intervals below zero. At 25 km, the association strengthened ($\beta=-0.12279$, 95\% CI $-0.12698$ to $-0.11860$; $\Delta R^2=0.013113$), again with 16/16 negative cohort estimates and 16/16 confidence intervals below zero. All five confirmatory criteria were satisfied. The classification of the information horizon at approximately 20 km was therefore unchanged when late-race performance was represented without an early-to-late ratio.

\section{Discussion}

This study developed and validated an order-sensitive functional of marathon pacing using only routine 5-km timing observations. The main finding was not simply that irregular pacing was associated with poorer late-race performance. Rather, the \emph{information carried by the evolving trajectory had a reproducible spatial onset}: little stable information was present at 15 km, a directionally consistent signal emerged across all 16 race-year cohorts by approximately 20 km, and the signal strengthened markedly by 25 km. This pattern survived performance stratification, comparison with conventional CV, and, critically, removal of the early-to-late ratio from the outcome. The latter result makes a purely algebraic explanation of the information horizon unlikely.

\subsection{From global pacing summaries to ordered local regulation}
The contemporary marathon literature increasingly treats pacing as a dynamic signal rather than a single half-race ratio \citep{Sha2024,Ristanovic2026}. CV, pace range, clustering, autocorrelation, and wall-detection rules have each expanded the description of race dynamics \citep{Smyth2021,Dong2026}. APRC addresses a narrower methodological gap: it preserves \emph{adjacency}. Two trajectories can contain the same set of segment velocities and therefore the same mean and CV, yet have different APRC values if those velocities occur in a different order. The measure therefore asks how rapidly the normalized operating state changes from one observed segment to the next.

The closest existing approaches occupy complementary measurement levels. Autocorrelation-based tracking preserves serial dependence in official split data \citep{Thuany2025}; critical-speed analysis anchors early intensity to an independently estimated physiological performance boundary \citep{SmythMuniz2020}; and functional analysis of IMU signals extracts early biomechanical signatures of subsequent severe slowing \citep{Miyazaki2025}. APRC differs in requiring only routine timing-mat observations while explicitly preserving adjacent split order and evaluating how information about a future, spatially separated outcome develops with race distance. Supplementary Table~S1 maps these methodological precedents.

The strong correlation between APRC and CV is consequently expected rather than problematic. Both respond to variability. The relevant validation question is whether preserving local ordering contributes information beyond global dispersion. The incremental $R^2$ beyond CV at 20 and 25 km supports that proposition, while the collinearity between the measures argues against interpreting their mutually adjusted coefficients as separable physiological effects.

\subsection{An information horizon rather than a fatigue threshold}
The approximately 20-km result should not be interpreted as the location of a universal physiological transition. It is an \emph{information horizon}: under the present measurement resolution, outcome definition, races, and population, this was the earliest tested distance at which the observed trajectory met a reproducibility criterion for subsequent performance.

The distinction is empirically important. In the primary ratio analysis, the enormous sample made the 15-km coefficient statistically detectable despite negligible incremental information and incomplete cohort agreement. In the de-coupled analysis, the 15-km coefficient even changed sign while $\Delta R^2$ approached zero and cohort direction was evenly divided. By contrast, the 20-km and 25-km conclusions were invariant to outcome construction at the level that matters for the proposed method: all 16 cohorts showed the same negative direction. Thus, statistical significance at one horizon was not used to define onset; reproducibility and information gain were.

The location of the information horizon is nevertheless physiologically plausible. Marathon pacing is constrained by interacting processes that evolve over prolonged exercise, including substrate availability, thermoregulatory and cardiovascular strain, neuromuscular fatigue, perceived effort, and the durability of sustainable intensity \citep{Azevedo2021}. Wearable studies have identified changes in running mechanics before or during substantial later-race slowing \citep{Meyer2021,Miyazaki2025}, while critical-speed work shows that early intensity relative to sustainable capacity is related to subsequent marathon deterioration \citep{SmythMuniz2020}. These findings provide biological context for a progressively informative trajectory but do not identify the mechanism underlying APRC.

\subsection{Relationship to critical-speed and power-duration physiology}
Critical-speed and power-duration models provide a mechanistic framework for the finite duration over which a given intensity can be sustained. Drake et al.\ showed that power-law formulations can represent endurance performance over a broad duration range and formalize the cost of unsustainable pace selection \citep{Drake2024}. More recent work in this journal has emphasized validation of power-duration models against independent performance observations \citep{WahlJi2026}. In recreational marathon runners, critical speed estimated from training data has also been related to race performance and pacing \citep{SmythMuniz2020}.

APRC is complementary rather than competing. It does not estimate an athlete's critical speed, physiological reserve, or metabolic power. Instead, it extracts an ordered structural property from the realized field trajectory. This distinction is necessary because converting velocity into metabolic power requires assumptions about running cost and, particularly during acceleration or changing terrain, additional mechanical and energetic terms \citep{Briand2025}. The current 5-km observations cannot support such reconstruction. A future validation study combining APRC with independently measured critical speed, running economy, physiological durability, or wearable-derived mechanics would therefore be more informative than retrospectively assigning a metabolic meaning to the present functional.

\subsection{Why outcome de-coupling matters}
Ratio-based metrics are common in marathon research because they offer intuitive normalization, but ratios can induce mathematical coupling and obscure whether an association arises from the numerator, denominator, or both \citep{Tenan2025}. This concern was especially relevant here because primary LCP contains early velocity in its denominator and APRC is normalized by early reference velocity.

The confirmatory analysis deliberately removed that dependency from the outcome. Absolute late-race velocity was modeled while conditioning explicitly on early velocity. The baseline already explained more than 80\% of its variance, leaving comparatively little residual information to recover. Even under this stringent specification, the spatial pattern remained: effectively no incremental information at 15 km, a replicated negative signal at 20 km, and a stronger signal at 25 km. The information-horizon classification was therefore robust to whether subsequent performance was represented relatively or absolutely.

\subsection{Field-methodological relevance}
The method requires no additional sensor, laboratory visit, or proprietary algorithm. It can be calculated from timing observations already generated by mass-participation races and, because it is horizon-specific, can be evaluated before the outcome window occurs. This makes it conceptually different from retrospective classifications based on the completed marathon.

The approach also aligns with a central principle of methods research: a useful derived metric should be transparent enough that its assumptions and failure modes remain visible \citep{Westerblad2025}. APRC has only three essential operations---normalization, adjacent differencing, and squaring. Its simplicity facilitates replication across datasets and makes it possible to distinguish mathematical behavior from physiological interpretation.

\subsection{Performance level and demographic variables}
Performance level is the most consistent correlate of marathon pacing in contemporary reviews: faster runners generally display smaller variability and less late-race deterioration \citep{Sha2024,Ristanovic2026}. Sex differences are also frequently reported, whereas age effects are smaller or less consistent. However, recent reanalysis has shown that demographic conclusions from real-world marathon data can be sensitive to data quality, model specification, and performance interactions \citep{Tenan2025}. We therefore treated sex and age as adjustment variables rather than mechanistic endpoints.

More relevant to the present validation is that the 25-km APRC association remained negative across all performance quartiles. The proposed functional therefore did not derive its information solely from contrasting very fast with very slow runners. Future studies should test whether within-athlete physiological characteristics explain variation in APRC among runners operating at similar relative intensity.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}
The study combines ecological validity with an explicit method-validation structure. Strengths include more than half a million observations, predefined quality-control rules, spatial separation between primary observation and outcome windows, replication across 16 race-year cohorts, performance-stratified analyses, comparison with a conventional variability metric, and a confirmatory outcome formulation designed specifically to remove ratio coupling.

The limitations define the scope of inference. First, 5-km timing mats are coarse measurements and cannot resolve short surges, aid-station effects, or rapid recovery. Higher-resolution kilometer or wearable streams may shift the estimated information horizon. Second, velocity is an external performance signal rather than a direct physiological measurement. The dataset lacks oxygen uptake, heart rate, lactate, body mass, substrate use, perceived exertion, training history, and individual critical speed. Third, course elevation and weather are not explicitly modeled. Boston and New York City in particular contain systematic terrain-induced velocity changes; replication across heterogeneous courses reduces the likelihood of a single-course artifact but does not remove this source of variation. Fourth, normalization still uses early-race velocity within APRC itself. The de-coupled analysis removes the ratio outcome and adjusts for early velocity, substantially reducing but not mathematically eliminating all dependence on early-race observations. Fifth, the analyses are observational and do not establish that experimentally reducing APRC would improve performance. Finally, the 20-km horizon is conditional on the tested checkpoint resolution, races, years, population, model, and operational criterion. External validation in independent races is required before treating 20 km as a transferable cut-point.

\subsection{Implications for future validation}
The next validation step should be physiological rather than statistical elaboration of the same dataset. Prospective studies could combine high-resolution split or wearable velocity with independently measured critical speed, running economy, heart rate, core or skin temperature, perceived exertion, and biomechanical markers. Such designs could determine whether changes in APRC track known changes in physiological durability and whether the information horizon shifts with course profile, environment, training status, or athlete-specific sustainable intensity. Out-of-sample validation in races not used for method development would also determine whether the operational horizon generalizes.

\section{Conclusion}
Routine marathon split times contain an ordered component of pacing regulation that is not captured completely by global variability. A simple quadratic functional of successive normalized velocity changes provided a transparent field-derived measure of that structure. Across 551,337 runners and 16 marathon-year cohorts, the trajectory contained essentially no reproducible information about subsequent late-race performance at 15 km, a consistent signal emerged by approximately 20 km, and information strengthened markedly by 25 km. The same horizon was recovered when subsequent performance was expressed as absolute late-race velocity conditional on early velocity, addressing a key ratio-coupling concern. The proposed information horizon is not a physiological threshold; it is a reproducible property of the measured pacing trajectory and a testable target for future physiological validation.
