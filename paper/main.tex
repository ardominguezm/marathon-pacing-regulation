\title{\textbf{Quantifying Pacing Regulation in Marathon Running: A Field-Derived Functional and Its Information Horizon}}

\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Purpose} Marathon split times are routinely collected under ecologically valid conditions, yet most pacing metrics compress the trajectory into global variability, categorical profiles, or early-to-late ratios. We developed an order-sensitive functional of local pacing regulation and determined the earliest race distance at which the observed trajectory contains reproducible information about subsequent late-race capacity preservation.

\textbf{Methods} We analyzed 5-km split records from 551,337 finishers of the Berlin, Boston, Chicago, and New York City Marathons across 16 race-year cohorts. Horizon-specific pacing regulation cost (APRC) was defined as the mean squared change between successive normalized segment velocities observed through 15, 20, or 25 km. Validation included race-year replication, performance strata, spatial progression, comparison with coefficient of variation (CV), and a confirmatory analysis replacing the ratio outcome with absolute late-race velocity conditional on early velocity.

\textbf{Results} At 15 km, APRC added negligible information ($\Delta R^2=0.00018$) and was negative in 11/16 cohorts. At 20 km, the association was negative in all 16 cohorts ($\beta=-0.00977$, 95\% CI $-0.01032$ to $-0.00922$; $\Delta R^2=0.00939$), and strengthened at 25 km ($\beta=-0.02575$, 95\% CI $-0.02663$ to $-0.02487$; $\Delta R^2=0.06288$). APRC added information beyond CV at 20 km ($\Delta R^2=0.02771$) and 25 km ($0.04294$). The information horizon was unchanged with absolute 30--40-km velocity conditional on early velocity: $\Delta R^2=0.00001$, 0.00227, and 0.01311 at 15, 20, and 25 km; associations were negative in 16/16 cohorts at both 20 and 25 km.

\textbf{Conclusion} A transparent quadratic functional derived from routine split times captured the ordered local structure of marathon pacing. Across four major marathons, a reproducible information signal about subsequent late-race performance emerged by approximately 20 km and strengthened markedly by 25 km. This information horizon is an empirical property of the observed pacing signal, not a universal physiological threshold.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} marathon running; pacing; endurance performance; fatigue resistance; field methods; real-world data

\section{Introduction}
Pacing is the continuously evolving regulation of work rate across a finite exercise task. In marathon running, the realized velocity trajectory reflects sustainable metabolic intensity, substrate availability, neuromuscular function, thermoregulatory strain, perception of effort, terrain, environmental conditions, tactical decisions, and expectations about sustainable intensity. Official timing mats repeatedly sample this trajectory under authentic competitive conditions, providing an unusually large field signal without additional instrumentation.

Most marathon studies compress this signal into positive/even/negative profiles, early-to-late percentage change, pace range, or CV. These approaches establish robust population regularities, but global dispersion discards ordering. Two runners may have the same set of segment velocities and similar CV while differing substantially in how abruptly and where those velocities change. Recent work has extended the field toward late-race wall definitions, serial-dependence tracking, critical-speed anchors, biomechanical early warning, and cross-edition pacing reproducibility.

The closest methodological precedents occupy complementary levels: tracking analyses preserve serial dependence in official splits; critical-speed approaches anchor early intensity to a physiological performance boundary; and IMU studies extract early biomechanical signatures of later slowing. To our knowledge, no previous split-time approach has defined an order-sensitive local regulation functional and used its progressive observation to determine a reproducible information horizon for subsequent marathon performance.

We introduce a quadratic functional of normalized segment-to-segment velocity changes. It is analogous to a discrete roughness or Dirichlet-type functional: small values represent locally smooth pacing, while larger values reflect sharper adjacent changes. The term \emph{cost} is mathematical, not calorimetric; APRC is not an estimate of metabolic energy expenditure, oxygen consumption, mechanical work, or thermodynamic entropy production.

We tested whether APRC contains reproducible information about subsequent late-race capacity preservation, when that information first becomes consistent across independent marathon-year cohorts, whether it persists across performance strata, and whether it adds information beyond conventional CV. Because ratio outcomes can generate mathematical coupling, we additionally specified a confirmatory analysis using absolute late-race velocity conditional on early velocity.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Study design and quality control}
We conducted a retrospective observational method-development study using public official race-result records from Berlin, Boston, Chicago, and New York City. Records included age, recorded sex, year, marathon, finish time, and cumulative 5-km timing observations. Primary analyses required age 15--80 years, finish time below 8 h, complete 5--40-km checkpoints, monotonically increasing cumulative times, segment velocities of 1.5--7.5 m s$^{-1}$, and adjacent-segment velocity ratios of 0.55--1.55. The primary pipeline retained 551,337 of 570,843 records (96.6\%).

\subsection{Segment velocity and pacing regulation cost}
For runner $i$ and segment $k$, $v_{ik}=\Delta x/\Delta t_{ik}$ with $\Delta x=5$ km. We defined
\begin{equation}v_{i,\mathrm{ref}}=\operatorname{median}(v_{i,10},v_{i,15},v_{i,20}),\qquad r_{ik}=v_{ik}/v_{i,\mathrm{ref}}.\end{equation}
For observation horizon $x$,
\begin{equation}\mathcal{R}_i(x)=\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{j=1}^{m(x)}[r_i(x_{j+1})-r_i(x_j)]^2.\label{eq:aprc}\end{equation}
Primary horizons were 15, 20, and 25 km. Squaring makes contributions non-negative and emphasizes larger local changes; averaging permits comparison across horizons. APRC is a trajectory-roughness functional and has no units of physiological energy.

\subsection{Outcome and information horizon}
Late-race capacity preservation (LCP) was mean segment velocity over 30--40 km divided by mean early-race velocity over 5--20 km. The 25-km horizon retained a 5-km spatial buffer before the outcome. Baseline models included standardized early reference velocity, age, age squared, sex, marathon, and year; standardized APRC was then added. Incremental information was $\Delta R^2=R^2_{\mathrm{baseline+APRC}}-R^2_{\mathrm{baseline}}$. Information onset was operationally defined as the earliest primary horizon with $\Delta R^2\ge0.005$, a negative APRC coefficient, and negative coefficients in at least 75\% of race-year cohorts. This is an empirical reproducibility criterion, not a physiological threshold.

\subsection{Validation and outcome de-coupling}
Validation included PRC progression across 10--20, 20--30, and 30--40 km; independent estimation in 16 race-year cohorts; performance quartiles; and comparison with same-horizon CV. Because APRC and CV are strongly correlated, conditional coefficient signs were not interpreted mechanistically. To address mathematical coupling from the ratio outcome, a final confirmatory analysis used absolute mean 30--40-km velocity as the outcome while explicitly adjusting for early reference velocity. Continuous predictors were standardized; OLS models used HC3 standard errors. Interpretation emphasized effect magnitude, incremental information, spatial ordering, and replication rather than $p$ values alone.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Spatial progression}
Mean PRC increased from 0.00186 over 10--20 km to 0.00328 over 20--30 km and 0.00381 over 30--40 km. Corresponding medians were 0.00045, 0.00127, and 0.00169; median late-race PRC was approximately 3.75 times the early-race value.

\subsection{Information horizon}
At 15 km, APRC had a small pooled association with LCP ($\beta=-0.00131$, 95\% CI $-0.00164$ to $-0.00098$) but negligible incremental information ($\Delta R^2=0.00018$) and only 11/16 negative cohort estimates. At 20 km, $\beta=-0.00977$ (95\% CI $-0.01032$ to $-0.00922$), $\Delta R^2=0.00939$, and all 16 cohorts were negative. At 25 km, $\beta=-0.02575$ (95\% CI $-0.02663$ to $-0.02487$), $\Delta R^2=0.06288$, again with 16/16 negative cohorts. The operational information horizon was therefore 20 km.

\subsection{Performance strata and information beyond CV}
The horizon-dependent increase persisted across performance quartiles, and the APRC coefficient at 25 km was negative in all four strata. APRC correlated strongly with same-horizon CV ($r=0.81$, 0.83, and 0.82 at 15, 20, and 25 km), but adding APRC to a model already containing CV increased $R^2$ by 0.00272, 0.02771, and 0.04294, respectively. This supports incremental information from ordered local changes beyond global dispersion.

\subsection{Confirmatory de-coupled outcome}
The baseline model for absolute 30--40-km velocity already explained $R^2=0.80579$. At 15 km, APRC added essentially no information ($\Delta R^2=0.000011$); the coefficient was small and positive ($\beta=0.00335$), and only 8/16 cohorts were negative. At 20 km, APRC was negative in all 16 cohorts ($\beta=-0.05014$, 95\% CI $-0.05302$ to $-0.04725$; $\Delta R^2=0.002267$). At 25 km, the association strengthened ($\beta=-0.12279$, 95\% CI $-0.12698$ to $-0.11860$; $\Delta R^2=0.013113$), again with 16/16 negative cohort estimates. Thus the information-horizon classification was unchanged after removal of the early-to-late ratio from the outcome.

\section{Discussion}
The main finding is methodological: a simple order-sensitive functional computed from routine 5-km split times captured an aspect of marathon pacing that became reproducibly informative about subsequent performance only after sufficient race distance had accumulated. Little stable information was present at 15 km; by approximately 20 km, all 16 race-year cohorts showed the same negative association; by 25 km, the signal was substantially stronger. This structure survived performance stratification, comparison with CV, and removal of the ratio outcome.

\subsection{Ordered local regulation versus global variability}
CV and APRC are related but not equivalent. CV measures dispersion without regard to sequence; APRC depends on adjacency. Reordering otherwise identical segment velocities can leave CV unchanged while changing APRC. The strong empirical correlation is expected, but the additional $R^2$ from APRC indicates that local ordering carries information beyond global variability. Because of collinearity, mutually adjusted coefficient signs should not be interpreted as distinct physiological effects.

\subsection{Information horizon, not physiological threshold}
The approximately 20-km onset is not evidence of a discrete biological transition at 20 km. It is an information horizon conditional on the present 5-km resolution, races, population, outcome, and reproducibility criterion. The 15-km analysis illustrates the distinction: a very large sample can produce a statistically resolved pooled coefficient before a signal is practically informative or reproducible. In the de-coupled outcome analysis, the 15-km coefficient even changed sign while $\Delta R^2$ approached zero, whereas the 20- and 25-km conclusions remained directionally unanimous across cohorts.

The spatial emergence is physiologically plausible without identifying a unique mechanism. Substrate availability, cardiovascular and thermoregulatory strain, neuromuscular fatigue, perceived effort, terrain, and durability of sustainable intensity all evolve during prolonged exercise. Critical-speed and wearable-biomechanics studies provide complementary physiological and mechanical anchors, but the present split data cannot separate these processes.

\subsection{Field-methodological relevance}
The method requires no laboratory visit, wearable, or proprietary algorithm. It uses timing observations already generated by mass-participation races and can be evaluated before the downstream outcome window occurs. Its operations are deliberately transparent: normalization, adjacent differencing, and squaring. This simplicity makes assumptions visible and facilitates replication across datasets.

The closest existing approaches occupy different measurement levels. Autocorrelation-based tracking preserves serial dependence in official split data; critical-speed analysis anchors early intensity to an independently estimated performance boundary; and functional IMU analysis extracts early biomechanical signatures of later slowing. APRC differs by using routine timing-mat observations while explicitly preserving adjacent split order and evaluating how information about a future spatially separated outcome develops with race distance.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}
Strengths include more than half a million observations, explicit quality control, spatial separation of observation and primary outcome windows, replication across 16 race-year cohorts, performance-stratified analyses, comparison with CV, and a confirmatory outcome formulation designed to reduce ratio coupling. Limitations include coarse 5-km resolution; absence of VO$_2$, heart rate, lactate, body mass, substrate use, perceived exertion, training history, and individual critical speed; unmodeled elevation and weather; residual dependence of APRC normalization on early velocity; observational design; and lack of independent external validation. Course topology is especially relevant for Boston and New York City. Replication across heterogeneous races reduces the likelihood of a single-course artifact but does not remove terrain effects.

The next validation step should therefore be physiological rather than further statistical elaboration of the same dataset. Prospective studies could combine high-resolution velocity with independently measured critical speed, running economy, heart rate, temperature, perceived exertion, and biomechanics, and test whether the information horizon shifts with course profile, environment, training status, or athlete-specific sustainable intensity.

\section{Conclusion}
Routine marathon split times contain an ordered component of pacing regulation not captured completely by global variability. Across 551,337 runners and 16 race-year cohorts, the trajectory contained essentially no reproducible information about subsequent late-race performance at 15 km, a consistent signal emerged by approximately 20 km, and information strengthened markedly by 25 km. The same horizon was recovered using absolute late-race velocity conditional on early velocity. The information horizon is not a physiological threshold; it is a reproducible property of the measured pacing trajectory and a testable target for future physiological validation.
